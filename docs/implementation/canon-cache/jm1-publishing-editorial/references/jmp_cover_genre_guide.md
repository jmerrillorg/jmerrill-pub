# jmp_cover_genre_guide
J Merrill Publishing Cover Genre Guide
Status: Recreated for the current Cover Intelligence GPT (August 2026). Not
a recovery of a prior artifact — built intentionally around the present
system. Attach as a Knowledge file to the JMP Cover Intelligence GPT.

Purpose: Provide the JMP Cover Intelligence GPT with genre-specific visual,
typography, color, positioning, and market-convention guidance when
developing book covers.

---

## 1. General Genre Rule

Every cover must achieve two objectives simultaneously:
1. Clearly signal the book's genre or subgenre to the intended reader.
2. Differentiate the title enough to avoid appearing generic or derivative.

Genre convention is a communication system, not a restriction.

The GPT should identify:
- Primary genre
- Secondary genre or subgenre
- Intended readership
- Emotional promise
- Comparable-title visual conventions
- Conventions that should be retained
- Conventions that may be intentionally broken

---

## 2. Fiction

**General Fiction**
Prioritize: strong emotional atmosphere, clear focal hierarchy, conceptual
imagery over excessive literalism, author and title visibility at
thumbnail size.
Avoid: generic stock photography, overly busy scenic compositions, imagery
that reveals plot unnecessarily.

**Literary Fiction**
Typical characteristics: restrained compositions, symbolic or conceptual
imagery, refined typography, muted/sophisticated/unusual palettes, strong
use of negative space.
Typography: serif or sophisticated display typography often performs well;
avoid excessively commercial treatment unless manuscript positioning
supports it.

**Romance**
Typical characteristics: clear emotional or relational signal, strong
color psychology, character-focused or symbolic treatment depending on
subgenre, highly readable title typography. Subgenre matters significantly
— contemporary, historical, romantic suspense, inspirational, urban. Avoid
applying one romance visual language universally.

**Thriller / Suspense**
Prioritize: tension, high contrast, bold typography, controlled darkness,
strong focal image or visual disruption.
Common palettes: black, deep blue, red accents, desaturated photography,
stark white contrast.
Avoid: visual clutter, weak title treatment, imagery that feels more
horror than suspense unless appropriate.

**Mystery**
Prioritize: intrigue, implied narrative, setting or object-based visual
clues, clear distinction between cozy, procedural, historical, or noir
mystery.

**Historical Fiction**
Prioritize: era accuracy, authentic wardrobe/architecture/objects/
environment, appropriate typography without becoming decorative pastiche,
emotional connection to the period. Never introduce anachronistic imagery.

**Fantasy**
Prioritize: world-building signal, distinctive iconography, strong
atmosphere, series scalability where relevant.
Avoid: generic fantasy landscapes, AI-looking magical imagery without
narrative specificity, excessive detail that disappears at thumbnail size.

**Science Fiction**
Prioritize: futuristic or speculative signal, strong conceptual
composition, technological/cosmic/societal/philosophical cues depending on
subgenre. Avoid defaulting every science-fiction title to spaceships or
planets.

## 3. Nonfiction

**General Nonfiction**
The cover must quickly communicate what the book is about, who it is for,
and why the reader should care. Typography often carries more of the
selling burden than imagery.

**Business / Leadership**
Prioritize: authority, confidence, simplicity, strong title hierarchy,
professional color systems.
Typical approaches: bold typography, geometric or conceptual visual
language, minimalism, strategic accent color.
Avoid: cheap corporate stock imagery, excessive iconography, template-like
business-book appearance.

**Self-Help / Personal Development**
Prioritize: clarity, transformation, accessibility, emotional optimism,
strong title visibility. Design should communicate possibility without
becoming generic motivational design.

**Memoir / Autobiography**
Manuscript authenticity moves higher in priority. Consider: author
likeness, authentic personal imagery, significant locations, symbolic
imagery tied directly to the life story. Do not invent visual elements
that materially misrepresent the subject's lived experience.

**Biography**
Balance: subject recognition, historical or cultural context, authority,
narrative tone.

**History**
Prioritize: credibility, era relevance, archival integrity, strong
hierarchy. Avoid sensational treatment unless the book's positioning
warrants it.

## 4. Faith / Christian / Inspirational

JMP should avoid default "Christian stock art." Use faith symbolism
intentionally.

Potential visual language: light, pathways, nature, hands, community,
architecture, personal transformation, scripture-inspired imagery, cross
imagery when narratively justified.

Avoid: random glowing crosses, overly artificial heavenly light, generic
praying silhouettes, visual stereotypes that reduce authenticity.

Consider: faith tradition, intended age group, gender mix of readership,
ministry versus trade-market positioning, whether the work is devotional,
theological, testimony, leadership, inspirational, or practical Christian
living.

Typography should feel appropriate to the audience rather than
automatically ornate or script-based.

---

## 5. Poetry

Prioritize: mood, voice, symbolism, visual restraint, emotional
atmosphere.
Common strengths: minimalism, restrained color palettes, conceptual
photography, illustration, typographic covers, vintage or fine-art
references where appropriate.
Avoid: over-explaining the poems through literal imagery, decorative
typography that compromises readability.

---

## 6. Children's Books

Age band must be identified before design. Consider separately: picture
books, early readers, middle grade, young adult.

For children's covers: character appeal, strong visual storytelling,
clear age signaling, energetic composition where appropriate, series
consistency.

Avoid designing a middle-grade title to look preschool or YA.

---

## 7. Young Adult

Prioritize: immediate emotional signal, strong trend awareness without
trend dependency, genre-specific typography, high thumbnail performance.

YA is an audience category, not a single genre. Design must still follow
the underlying genre.

---

## 8. Genre Color Intelligence

Color choices should be informed by: genre conventions, emotional tone,
audience, competitive shelf environment, brand or series needs.

For every project, identify: dominant palette, accent color, background
strategy, emotional color rationale, colors prevalent in competing titles,
colors that could create useful differentiation, colors that may
accidentally signal the wrong genre.

---

## 9. Comparable Cover Analysis

Where comparable-title research is available, identify 3-5 relevant
titles and evaluate: dominant imagery, typography, title scale,
author-name prominence, color palette, use of photography or illustration,
composition, subtitle handling, endorsement usage.

Then explicitly state:
- **Borrow from convention:** What visual language should be recognizable
  to the target reader?
- **Differentiate:** What should JMP do differently enough to create
  distinction?

Comparable titles should guide strategy, not be copied.

---

## 10. Series Rule

If a title belongs to a series, establish a repeatable system for: title
lockup, series identifier, author placement, typography, spine treatment,
imprint placement, image treatment, color variation, volume numbering.

The first cover should be designed as the beginning of a system, not as an
isolated object.
